# Chapter 11

## The Five Failure Modes

### (A Complete Diagnostic System for the Instrument)

---

### Narration

By now you have learned how to find baseline, detect drift, regulate
gain, recognize craving, and see the ego as a directional process
rather than a self.

The instrument is becoming precise.

But precision creates a new problem.

The more sensitive the instrument, the more clearly you can see when
it is not working correctly.

And there are exactly five ways it fails.

Not metaphorically five.
Not approximately five.

Five.

They have been mapped for twenty-five hundred years under different
names in different traditions. Here they appear as what they actually
are:

Engineering failure modes.

Each one has a specific signature.
Each one has a specific corrective.
And none of them are moral failures.

They are system states.

When you can recognize which failure mode is active and apply the
correct corrective, the instrument recovers.

That is the whole chapter.

---

### Dialogue

**Student:**
So there are only five ways the instrument can fail?

**Teacher:**
Five distinct modes, yes.

Everything else is a combination or a consequence.

**Student:**
That seems too simple.

**Teacher:**
The system is not complicated.

It is subtle.

Those are different things.

---

### The Diagnostic Question

**Teacher:**
Before we name them, learn the diagnostic question.

When something feels off, ask:

> *What is the instrument doing right now?*

Not: what am I feeling?
Not: what does this mean?
Not: what should I do?

Just: what is the instrument doing?

The answer will always be one of five things.

---

### Failure Mode One — Approach Gain Running Hot

**Student:**
What's the first one?

**Teacher:**
The system is pulling toward something.

A pleasant sensation.
An interesting thought.
A desirable state.
A person, memory, or outcome.

Gain is being applied in the approach direction and it is above the
workable threshold.

**Student:**
How does that feel?

**Teacher:**
Restless. Hungry. Reaching.

Sometimes it feels like excitement that won't settle.
Sometimes it feels like obsession.
Sometimes it feels pleasant but keeps escalating.

The signature is: *something keeps pulling the cursor forward.*

**Student:**
And the corrective?

**Teacher:**
Reduce approach gain.

Soften the grip.
Let the object remain without reaching toward it.
Release the forward lean.

You are not destroying the pleasant object.
You are withdrawing the amplification.

**Student:**
That sounds like suppression.

**Teacher:**
Suppression pushes the object away.
This just removes the pull.

The object can still be there.
You simply stop amplifying it.

---

### Failure Mode Two — Avoidance Gain Running Hot

**Teacher:**
The second failure mode is the mirror image.

The system is pushing away from something.

An unpleasant sensation.
An uncomfortable thought.
A state you don't want to be in.
A memory, anticipation, or sensation that feels threatening.

Gain is being applied in the avoidance direction and it is above the
workable threshold.

**Student:**
How does that feel?

**Teacher:**
Tight. Resistant. Braced.

Sometimes it feels like low-grade irritation.
Sometimes it feels like dread.
Sometimes it feels like the system is holding its breath.

The signature is: *something keeps pushing the cursor backward.*

**Student:**
And the corrective?

**Teacher:**
Reduce avoidance gain.

Soften the resistance.
Let the unpleasant object remain without pushing against it.
Release the backward lean.

**Student:**
That also sounds like forcing yourself to accept something bad.

**Teacher:**
Acceptance in the forced sense is still gain — just disguised.

This is not acceptance.

It is the withdrawal of resistance.

The object can remain exactly as it is.
You simply stop amplifying the push.

---

### The Symmetry

**Teacher:**
Notice the symmetry between the first two modes.

Same mechanism.
Opposite direction.
Same corrective: reduce gain.

This is why craving and aversion feel so different but are structurally
identical.

Both are the instrument applying force to a formation.
Both destabilize in the same way.
Both respond to the same basic move.

**Student:**
So the corrective for both is just... less?

**Teacher:**
Less amplification.

Not less experience.
Not less feeling.
Not less sensation.

Less gain on the formation.

The experience can be as vivid as it is.
The gain is what you withdraw.

---

### Failure Mode Three — System Under-Energized

**Teacher:**
The third failure mode is different in character.

The system is below its minimum operating threshold.

Not being pulled anywhere.
Not being pushed anywhere.

Just... low.

**Student:**
How does that feel?

**Teacher:**
Heavy. Foggy. Dull.

Thoughts move slowly or not at all.
The body feels dense.
Attention keeps sliding off objects.
Sleep pressure increases.
Everything feels distant or muffled.

The signature is: *the instrument cannot hold an object.*

**Student:**
And the corrective?

**Teacher:**
Add energy carefully.

The key word is carefully.

The most common mistake here is over-correcting — adding too much
energy and tipping into Failure Mode Four.

Safe ways to add energy:

- Mild isometric tension in the body
- Gentle interest directed at a simple object
- Slightly more precise attention
- Opening the eyes if they are closed
- Returning to ordinary activity if the system won't stabilize

Interest is the cleanest tool.

Not effort. Not force. Not stimulation.

Just a genuine noticing of something specific.

**Student:**
Why not just drink coffee?

**Teacher:**
You can.

But caffeine adds energy at the chemistry layer without giving you
control of where it goes.

Interest adds energy precisely where the instrument is aimed.

---

### Failure Mode Four — System Over-Energized

**Teacher:**
The fourth failure mode is the opposite.

The system is above its maximum stable threshold.

**Student:**
How does that feel?

**Teacher:**
Agitated. Scattered. Pressured.

Thoughts move too fast.
Attention jumps between objects without completing a cycle.
The body feels tight or buzzy.
There is a sense of urgency without a clear object.
Concentration feels impossible.

The signature is: *the instrument cannot stay with an object.*

**Student:**
That sounds like anxiety.

**Teacher:**
Anxiety is often this failure mode with narration added.

The energetic signature is over-energization.
The story layer adds interpretation.

Treat the energetic layer first.

**Student:**
And the corrective?

**Teacher:**
Reduce system energy.

Release excess tension in the body.
Soften breath.
Widen attention rather than narrowing it.
Let the system slow without forcing it.

The Seven Factors apply here directly:

When the system is too high, emphasize:
relaxation, concentration, equanimity.

When the system is too low, emphasize:
investigation, persistence, interest.

Mindfulness monitors which is needed.

**Student:**
What if I can't tell which direction I'm off?

**Teacher:**
Use the relaxation diagnostic from Chapter 2.

Relax and observe.

If the system stabilizes — you were over-energized.
If the system collapses — you were under-energized.

One move. Clear answer.

---

### Failure Mode Five — Volitional Seizure

**Teacher:**
The fifth failure mode is the most dangerous and the least discussed.

**Student:**
What is it?

**Teacher:**
The decision mechanism locks.

The tri-state system — approach, avoid, let pass — becomes
simultaneously unavailable.

The system cannot move forward.
Cannot move backward.
Cannot release.

**Student:**
What does that feel like?

**Teacher:**
It presents as doubt.

Not ordinary uncertainty.

Catastrophic doubt.

The kind that says:

*Nothing I do is right.*
*I cannot know anything for certain.*
*Every option is equally wrong.*
*I should not move until I am sure — and I can never be sure.*

The system freezes.

**Student:**
That sounds like anxiety too.

**Teacher:**
It has a similar surface feeling.

But the signature is different.

Anxiety is over-energization with an object.
Something specific feels threatening.

Volitional seizure has no clear object.
The paralysis is about moving at all.

The system is not afraid of a specific thing.
The system cannot execute a decision.

**Student:**
Why is this the most dangerous?

**Teacher:**
Because it masquerades as wisdom.

It looks like careful consideration.
It sounds like humility.
It feels like thoroughness.

But functionally it is seizure.

Heat builds without processing.
Accumulation without release.
The feedback loop cannot complete a cycle.

**Student:**
And the corrective?

**Teacher:**
A small, reversible action.

Any action.

Not the right action.
Not the perfect action.
Not the action you will never regret.

A small action that can be undone.

The volitional mechanism needs to complete one cycle to break the
seizure. The content of the action is almost irrelevant. The
completion is what matters.

**Student:**
What if I make a mistake?

**Teacher:**
You probably will.

That is acceptable.

A mistake with consequences is recoverable.
A seized system accumulating heat indefinitely is not.

Move. Adjust. Move again.

---

### The Complete Diagnostic System

**Teacher:**
Here is the full map.

When something feels off, ask: *what is the instrument doing?*

The answer is always one of these:

**1. Approach gain running hot**
Signature: pulling, reaching, restless escalation
Corrective: reduce approach gain, soften the grip

**2. Avoidance gain running hot**
Signature: tight, braced, resistant
Corrective: reduce avoidance gain, release the push

**3. System under-energized**
Signature: heavy, foggy, dull, attention slides
Corrective: add energy carefully — interest, gentle engagement

**4. System over-energized**
Signature: scattered, pressured, attention jumps
Corrective: reduce energy — relax, soften, widen attention

**5. Volitional seizure**
Signature: paralysis masquerading as careful doubt
Corrective: small reversible action to complete one cycle

---

### The Relationship Between Failure Modes

**Student:**
Can more than one be active at the same time?

**Teacher:**
Yes.

Failure modes stack.

A common combination: over-energized system with approach gain
running hot.

That is the signature of obsession.

Another: under-energized system with volitional seizure.

That is the signature of depression.

Another: avoidance gain running hot with over-energization.

That is the signature of panic.

**Student:**
So anxiety, obsession, panic, and depression are just
combinations of five failure modes?

**Teacher:**
At the mechanical level, yes.

That does not mean they are simple to work with.
It means they are workable.

**Student:**
That actually helps.

**Teacher:**
Because it removes the mystery.

When something is mysterious it feels permanent.
When something is mechanical it feels addressable.

---

### Mindfulness as the Monitor

**Teacher:**
One factor stands outside the five failure modes.

Mindfulness.

Mindfulness is not one of the five states.
It is the function that detects which state is present.

Without mindfulness, the failure modes run invisibly.

With mindfulness, they become visible the moment they appear.

This is why mindfulness is described as the monitor of the whole
system — not as a state to achieve but as the instrument's
self-awareness function.

When mindfulness is present and a failure mode arises, you notice.

When you notice, you can apply the corrective.

When you apply the corrective, the instrument recovers.

That is the complete loop.

---

### Safety Note

**Teacher:**
One clarification before we close.

Recognizing a failure mode does not mean forcing it to stop.

The corrective is always gentle.

If approach gain is running hot, you do not violently suppress the
reaching. You soften it.

If the system is over-energized, you do not force it down. You
release excess tension and let it settle.

If volitional seizure is present, you do not berate yourself for
being paralyzed. You take one small action.

Force is what created the problem in most cases.

Gentleness is the corrective in almost every case.

**Student:**
What if gentleness doesn't work?

**Teacher:**
Return to baseline externally.

Stop practice.
Return to ordinary activity.
Let the system reset through normal living.

Nothing essential is lost by stopping.

Everything essential is lost by forcing.

---

### Closing Narration

The instrument has five failure modes.

You now know all five.
You now know their signatures.
You now know their correctives.

This changes your relationship to difficulty in practice.

Difficulty is no longer mysterious.
It is no longer a sign that something is wrong with you.
It is no longer evidence that you cannot do this.

It is a diagnostic signal.

When something feels off, the question is no longer:

*What is wrong with me?*

It is:

*Which failure mode is active right now?*

And then:

*What is the corrective?*

That is all.

The instrument drifts.
You notice.
You apply the corrective.
The instrument recovers.

You continue.
